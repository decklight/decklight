The night the cache fell over

A postmortem of the 2:14am Redis outage

This is the story of the night our Redis cache fell over, at fourteen minutes past two in the morning. [pause] No blame here, just what happened, why it hurt so much, and what we changed.

Timeline

All times local, night of the incident

  • 02:14 — Redis primary OOM-killed; p99 alert fires
  • 02:16 — failover to a cold replica; every read misses
  • 02:31 — Postgres connection pool exhausted; errors climb
  • 02:47 — miss traffic shed, cache warms back up
  • 03:20 — p99 back under 100 ms; incident closed

Here's how the night unfolded.

[click]

At two fourteen, the Redis primary ran out of memory and the kernel killed it. Our p99 latency alert fired a few seconds later.

[click]

Two minutes after that, failover promoted a replica. But it came up cold, so every single read was a miss.

[click]

All of those misses went straight to Postgres, and by two thirty-one its connection pool was exhausted. That's when errors, not just slowness, started reaching users.

[click]

At two forty-seven we started shedding miss traffic, which finally let the cache warm back up.

[click]

And by three twenty, p99 was back under a hundred milliseconds and we closed the incident. About an hour, end to end.

Only on a miss do we fall through to Postgres. [pause] That night, with a cold replica, every read took this dashed path at once. Postgres was sized for the misses, not for all the traffic.

The fix

One thing that night, three things since

That night

  • Shed cache-miss reads at the API with a feature flag
  • Let the replica warm, then ramp traffic back

Since then

  • Single-flight fills: one DB query per missing key
  • Circuit breaker in front of Postgres
  • Memory headroom alert at 75% of maxmemory

On the night itself, the fix was blunt. We flipped a flag that made the API stop sending cache-miss reads to the database, served stale or degraded responses instead, and let the replica fill up before ramping traffic back.

[click]

Since then, we've made three permanent changes. First, single-flight fills: when a thousand requests miss on the same key, only one of them goes to the database, and the rest wait for its answer.

[click]

Second, a circuit breaker in front of Postgres, so an overloaded database fails fast instead of queueing everyone.

[click]

And third, an alert when Redis passes seventy-five percent of its memory limit, so we hear about it long before the kernel does.

Three lessons

  1. A cache is a dependency. Size what's behind it for the day it's empty.
  2. Failover isn't recovery. A cold replica is up, not ready.
  3. Alert on headroom, not on death. Memory told us hours before it killed us.

So, three lessons.

[click]

One. A cache is a dependency, not an optimisation. Whatever sits behind it has to survive the day the cache is empty.

[click]

Two. Failover is not recovery. Our replica was up within two minutes, but it wasn't ready for another half hour.

[click]

And three. Alert on headroom, not on death. Redis memory had been climbing for hours before it fell over. [pause] We just weren't listening. Thank you.
